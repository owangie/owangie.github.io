The project was tagged "indigenous people". Its funding proposal said: *"This project will be carried out in areas where there are no indigenous people."*

That was FP176, a hydro-agricultural project in Niger, and it was not the only one. For the [evaluation of the Green Climate Fund's approach to Indigenous Peoples](https://ieu.greenclimate.fund/sites/default/files/document/250221-ips-final-report-top-web_0.pdf), I worked on the portfolio and data analysis. Before we could say anything about how projects treat Indigenous Peoples, we had to answer a harder question: which projects actually involve them?

## Finding the right projects

The Fund tags its projects, and part of that tagging is done with AI tools. The tags are a useful start and an unreliable finish. Cross-checking them against the proposals turned up contradictions ([Annex II](https://ieu.greenclimate.fund/sites/default/files/document/annex-ii-ips-data-sources-and-methodology-top-2.pdf)):

- **FP176, Niger:** tagged for Indigenous Peoples, yet the proposal says the project does not affect them and runs where there are none.
- **FP068, Georgia:** no known Indigenous Peoples or ethnic groups in the area; stakeholder engagement only to confirm there is no impact.
- **FP182, Colombia:** no direct impact on indigenous reservations, with an ethnic differential approach in the risk analysis.

Words were the other trap. Some organisations treat Indigenous Peoples and ethnic groups as different concepts. Some governments do not recognise Indigenous Peoples at all and write "distinct groups" or "local communities" instead, terms that may or may not include them. And some readiness documents were in local languages or not machine readable.

So we did not trust the keyword hits. We cross-checked each one, added other variables, compared against secondary data, and narrowed the list to **128 highly relevant projects**. It is a judgement call, and the evaluation says so, but it is a documented one.

## What happens to women in those projects

Of the 128 projects, only **83 (66%)** discussed women in their Gender Action Plans at all. For those 83, we looked at how women appear in the activities projects report each year.

The method is simple and worth stating plainly. From the annual performance reports for 2022 and 2023, we took the paragraphs reporting progress on each project's Gender Action Plan. A keyword search sorted each reported activity into one of three categories:

1. women included in **capacity building or training**,
2. women's **share of benefits**,
3. women included in **decision-making**.

Each count was divided by the total number of reported activities in its theme, giving a proportion that can be compared across categories, then placed on a scale from 0 to 1.

Women were far more often trained (0.54) than given a share of the benefits (0.24) or a say in decisions (0.22). Being in the training room is not the same as being in the room where decisions are made.

## What the numbers can and cannot say

Two honest caveats. These scores cover the whole of each project, not only Indigenous women. And a keyword count measures what projects *report*, not what happened on the ground: a project that trains women and writes about it scores higher than one that gives women real power and describes it in different words.

That is why this kind of count is a starting point for questions in interviews and field visits, not a verdict. It points to where to look.

## What I took away

- Tags, including AI-generated ones, need a human cross-check before they define a sample.
- The words used for a group can hide whether the group is there at all.
- Count carefully, then ask whether the thing you counted is the thing that matters.
