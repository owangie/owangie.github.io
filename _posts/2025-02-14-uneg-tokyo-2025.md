I did not know the UN had a university in the middle of Tokyo until I walked into it. The United Nations University hosted UNEG's Evaluation Week in February 2025, three days of evaluators from across the UN system, plus academics and local researchers, arguing about how we know what works.

## Day one: AI, and who checks it

UNICEF opened with lessons from using large language models, topic modelling and text mining to analyse 302 evaluation reports, drawn from a wider pool of 1,300. The models found themes that people had overlooked. They also made things up, which is why the speaker kept coming back to the same point: you need thematic experts watching the output, or the synthesis stops being trustworthy. Running the analysis several times helped sharpen the framework, but questions about privacy and bias never went away. Their advice was to use locally hosted models and a proper quality assurance process, with qualitative tools such as MAXQDA alongside.

These were the same lessons I had been learning the slow way on my own projects: make the model quote its evidence, keep people reviewing the doubtful cases, and keep sensitive documents inside your own walls.

The same day, a panel on **national evaluation capacity development** tried to pull a scattered set of UN commitments into one strategy. The uncomfortable fact: few countries have ever evaluated their own capacity building, so the evidence on what works is thin. Culturally sensitive and gender responsive evaluation came up as the biggest gaps, along with a mismatch between global priorities and local realities, and not enough money.

A session from FAO, UNICEF and UNIDO on evaluating **South-South cooperation** hit close to home, since I had spent 2024 building a trust fund dashboard for the UN Office for South-South Cooperation. They talked about system change, such as a school feeding programme that buys food from local farmers, and about everything that makes it hard to measure: missing data, political sensitivity, surveys nobody answers. Their answer was portfolio analysis, better monitoring, and delivering findings diplomatically without bending them.

## Day two: climate, and training evaluators

The climate evaluation session covered the full toolkit, from randomised trials and systematic reviews to contribution analysis, big data and qualitative comparative analysis. The Adaptation Fund talked about ex-post evaluations: going back after a project closes to see what lasted, looking at ownership, resources, capacities and partnerships. Data quality, selection bias and time lags were the usual suspects. FAO showed, with its Nigeria country programme evaluation, why the results chain has to be set early so questions and methods line up. UNEP linked research literature to policy with mixed methods.

A later session on professionalising evaluation was frank about money: training evaluators is expensive, and budgets are tight. The [UNEG Evaluation Training Certificate Course](https://www.unevaluation.org/training/uneg-evaluation-training-certificate-course) came up, and it seems fewer people finish it than you would hope. If you are early in your career or new to the UN, it looks worth a try.

## Day three: our turn

On the last day, the professional development seminar, our LORTA team, Susumu Yoshida and Marco D'errico, presented *Advancing Impact Evaluation: Challenges, Insights, and Collaborative Solutions* to senior evaluators from FAO, UNDP, UNFPA, UNICEF and GEF, and researchers from academia. My part was behind the scenes: a slide that explains the counterfactual, using rainwater harvesting and access to clean water, and the interactive Mentimeter questions that let the room answer in real time.

The discussion was honest. Everyone agreed impact evaluations are useful and that they are expensive, short of data and hard to time. How agencies protect participants' data varied a lot: some have formal ethics review boards, some rely on confidentiality agreements, some do not run impact evaluations at all. A guest from Sophia University made the case for regression discontinuity as an alternative to randomised trials, and ADBI showed an Indonesian study on indoor air pollution where government buy-in made the difference.

Then the poll. At the start, 71% of the room said impact evaluation was a critical tool. By the end, 93% did. A small room, and a friendly one, but I will take it.

## What I brought home

- AI can widen what an evaluation sees, but only with experts checking what it finds.
- The evidence gap is not only in projects. It is in how we build evaluation capacity itself.
- A good question to the audience does more than another slide.
